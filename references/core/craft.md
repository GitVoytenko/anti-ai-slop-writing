# Core module: second-order craft rules (all languages)

Standalone module: edit independently of everything else. Always load this alongside the language modules — these rules apply to every language. They came out of editorial review of real outputs: texts that passed every word-list check and still read as generated. Word lists catch vocabulary; these catch pacing, ordering, and endings.

**One markedly short sentence per semantic block.** Liveliness and humor live in the length drop, not in the joke: a long detailed run-up, then a short hit with no explanation. Three evenly sized sentences in a row sound like a report even when every word is clean. In each block of the text, make one sentence conspicuously shorter than its neighbors — on purpose, not by luck.

**Detail over evaluation — and never explain a landed line.** An evaluation ("a serious concern", «возражение серьёзное») gets skimmed; a detail («это вам не техподдержка мобильного оператора») makes the reader draw the conclusion himself, and readers like feeling smart. Corollary: after a line that lands, stop. The most common way to kill a good sentence is the next sentence that decodes it. Said it — full stop, new thought.

**Let the author show, doubts included.** An impersonal recap of facts reads like a slide deck, and the reader smells a pitch. One honest admission of a limit — "we didn't measure that", «я бы не делал из одной студии закон», «вилізло збоку те, чого ніхто не закладав» — gives the text a human intonation faster than any joke. Admissions of scope and uncertainty are voice, not facts: you may honestly bound a claim, but never invent an event or a doubt that didn't happen (see Hard Rule in SKILL.md).

**End once.** Find the strongest closer and cut everything after it — every extra ending drains the one before. Watch for the stacked-finals pattern: a paragraph that closes the piece, then another that closes it again, then a tidy takeaway with a bow on it. Rhetorical self-questions as closers ("Would I do it again?", «Що далі?») are genre furniture; cut them.

**Don't announce conclusions.** "The lesson here is...", «Вывод простой:», «Отже, ...» — if the numbers and details already did the work, announcing the conclusion weakens it: the reader got there first and now feels lectured. An insight that emerges from the arithmetic beats one delivered in a wrapper. If the point doesn't land without the announcement, the specifics were too weak — fix those, not the framing.

**Order = priorities.** The reader takes the sequence of paragraphs as your ranking of what matters. In emails and announcements, the thing that affects the reader goes first ("your price stays until Dec 1"), your reasons after — or not at all. Announcing intentions is the same failure at sentence level: «коротко і по суті» is a promise of brevity instead of brevity. Don't announce — do.

**A decision states itself; long justification reads as weakness.** A price change, a shutdown, a policy — state it as fact with at most one reason, the one that benefits the reader ("the same team stays on your project"), not the list of your costs. A paragraph of justification is argumentation for an internal meeting that leaked into a client letter.

**One softener maximum.** "just", «просто», "feel free to", «розберемо разом», gratitude formulas — each one shrinks the message, and stacked at the end they read as apology. A calm message ends with a date and an action, not with thanks for the reader still being there.

**Give the number that hurts.** If the piece runs on specifics (14 months, 91 users, $6), don't go vague exactly at the uncomfortable spot — the refund total, the failed metric. That's where the reader feels the flinch, and one figure there weighs more than all the arithmetic above it. If the painful number isn't in the source material, don't write around it silently: in generation mode, ask the user for it or leave a visible placeholder («[сумма?]») for them to fill; inventing it is worse than either (Hard Rule).

**Vary paragraph mechanics, not just sentence lengths.** Flat opening claim + short tail comment, repeated in every paragraph, is a template even when each instance is good. If two consecutive paragraphs share the same internal shape, rebuild one of them.

## Revision discipline: rules must not eat the text

Applying everything above can over-tighten a draft until it reads honest but airless. Density is a failure mode too — a reader gives up on a physically hard paragraph before he ever meets your argument. These rules protect what the others tend to destroy.

**One paragraph — one thought, and the period comes earlier than feels natural.** Ease of reading is not short words; it's how many thoughts the reader must hold at once. The moment a second theme starts, that's a paragraph break — even mid-argument. A pause between thoughts is part of the argument.

**A scene beats its description.** Two lines of quoted speech («А в пятницу есть окошко?», «а можно перенести на час позже?») let the reader hear the voice and see the evening; the generalization they illustrate («переписка — часть сервиса») tells him what he could have seen himself. When you have a scene, never trade it for its summary. And keep more than one voice alive: if the piece is about a conflict between two sides, both must be heard.

**The key number gets a main clause.** Never park the central fact in a subordinate construction («пока она сомневалась, администратор проводила три-четыре часа...») — parked there, it services someone else's sentence and stops ringing. Give it its own sentence, then hit it once more, short, without explaining: «По три-четыре часа в день. Каждый день.» The re-hit may escalate instead of just repeating: the text's single allowed exclamation mark belongs exactly here, at the emotional peak, and a scope-widening tail hits harder than a bare echo — «Каждый день! И это только переписка.»

**Promise, not threat.** The reason for bad news describes what the reader keeps («the same people stay on your project»), never what you would otherwise take away («иначе — за рахунок якості»). The second reads as "so you could have"; the reader files it against you.

**Subject and body divide the work.** The subject line (or headline) states the news; the body answers the question the subject can't: what to do and by when. If the reader meets the same fact three times — subject, opening, recap — two of those are padding.

**The reader's one real option survives every edit.** If there's a concrete path for the person the news hits hardest (trim the scope instead of leaving; downgrade instead of canceling), it is the most valuable sentence in the piece — it cannot be a casualty of tightening. Phrase that call to action as a recommendation, not a command: «варто відповісти на цей лист до кінця листопада» reads as advice, «відповідайте» reads as an order — in a bad-news letter the deadline does the urging, the verb doesn't have to. Same for intonation: greeting and closing must match the news («Привіт!» with an exclamation mark doesn't open a price increase), and a letter needs an ending — brevity is not amputation.

**Emotional load-bearing lines are immovable.** Before trimming, mark the one or two lines that show why it mattered and what it cost («felt better than any performance review»). Cut those and a story becomes someone else's case study: the reader learns why the project died but not what it was like to kill it. Trim around them, never through them.

**One qualifier, one appearance.** A repeated condition ("on ten hours a week", three times) turns from context into excuse by the third mention. Say it once — where it explains the arithmetic, not where it excuses the result. And don't add polite escape hatches ("maybe a full-time founder would have...") that hand back the responsibility the text just took.
